# Static Ad Playbook — iMeow

**Niche:** Cat pet tech — for cat parents who can't be home
**Format that wins in this account:** plain background, bold direct headline, feature elements, targeting a specific avatar and situation
**What loses in this account:** native statics, native video, UGC talking to camera — all high CPC, low CTR, no conversions

> **Before using this document:** fill in the product spec below. Every prompt in Part 3 is a template with slots — do not write creative against features that haven't been confirmed.

```
PRODUCT:
PRICE:
CONFIRMED FEATURES:
FEATURES IT DOES NOT HAVE (never imply these):
COMPARE-AT PRICE (must be live on the PDP before any discount ad runs):
```

---

## Part 1 — The research this is built on

### The window
Facebook's own data: average mobile feed dwell time is **1.7 seconds** before someone moves on. That's the entire budget.

### Statics have two hooks, not one
1. **The image** — stops the scroll
2. **The primary text** — pulls them in

Both have to work. The image buys you the first line of copy; the copy does the selling. Build them together, never separately.

### What stops scroll
- **Curiosity gaps** — open a question the brain needs to close
- **Loss aversion** — what they're currently losing
- **Pattern interrupt** — something that reads differently from the branded-template statics wallpapering the feed
- **Contrast** — two opposing realities side by side
- **Social proof** — the slowest of the five to fake, which is why it still works

Hype and vague superlatives are invisible now. A precise, verifiable-sounding claim with an implied mechanism creates enough cognitive friction to stop the scroll.

### What kills a static
- No hook and no text — the single worst-performing format
- Too many messages — trying to say everything
- Everything emphasized — when everything is emphasized, nothing is

### Cat-owner specific triggers (ranked)
1. **Pain agitation** — dominates. Amplify the problem before offering relief.
2. **Identity call-out** — direct address to "cat dads," "multi-cat owners," "new cat parents" creates immediate self-selection.
3. **Curiosity gap** — blurred imagery, "what if I told you," warning-sign framings stop scrolling before the product is even mentioned.

**Named primary pain:** guilt around pet care when busy or away.

### The mechanism underneath the guilt
Research on pet owners found that the more owners attribute human-like mental abilities to their pet, the more they engage in **"making-up behavior"** — extra treats, a new toy, extra attention, even apologizing — specifically when they feel guilty about not giving the pet enough attention.

**This product category is a making-up device.** Ads that name the guilt and offer the repair pull a lever that already exists in the buyer. That's the single most important finding in this document.

### Pet tech is a considered purchase
Trackers, feeders and cameras have longer decision cycles than food or toys. Educational content and comparison-with-alternatives support the extended journey. So some ads must **explain**, not just agitate — especially at a premium price.

## Part 2 — The slate structure

One ad set, 4–6 ads, each doing a different job. Anyone who sees two or three gets walked forward regardless of the order Meta serves them in.

**The rule that makes it work:** every ad must convert standing alone. Most people will only ever see one. No ad may depend on another.

| # | Job | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Identity hook | Self-selection — names the buyer, not the product |
| 2 | Problem hook | Makes them feel the gap before they know what fixes it |
| 3 | Curiosity hook | Opens a question the copy answers. Highest scroll-stop, lowest explanation |
| 4 | Mechanism | Proves it's real tech, not a toy. Justifies the price |
| 5 | Comparison | Kills the "I already have a pet cam" objection |
| 6 | Closer | Price, offer, social proof. Asks for the sale |

### Price belongs in the closer, not everywhere
Only the closer carries the price on the image. Hook and explainer ads stay price-free — a number in the frame invites a buy-or-skip decision before they understand what it is, and at a premium price most cold viewers skip. Let the image and headline earn the click; the price lives on the landing page and in the closer.

### Where the funnel actually lives
The image is the scroll-stop. **The primary text is where the stage work happens.**
- Ads 1–3: open with the pain or question, close with a soft CTA
- Ads 4–5: explain, close with the offer
- Ad 6: lead with the offer

### Known constraint
You cannot sequence ads inside one ad set. Meta serves whichever it predicts will convert, and it will pick a winner and starve the rest — often a hook ad rather than the closer. That's fine if it converts. If the closer needs guaranteed delivery, it goes in its own ad set. That's the only lever Meta gives you.

Start with 4–6 ads. More than that and each gets too little spend to produce a readable result.

Bottom: black rounded price block with "[PRICE]" in large glowing white type. Small grey social-proof line under the price. Minimal, high-contrast, premium DTC layout.

## Part 4 — How to write more of these

### The template
```
[PRODUCT ON PLAIN BACKGROUND, angle, lighting, shadow/glow]
+ [HEADLINE — one trigger, 4–8 words, one emphasis effect]
+ [OPTIONAL SUBLINE — grey, small, explains the mechanism]
+ [OPTIONAL FEATURE ROW — 3–4 items, single line]
+ [PRICE BLOCK — closers only]
```

### Step 1 — Pick one trigger
Never two. One ad, one job.
- Pain agitation
- Identity call-out
- Curiosity gap
- Contrast
- Loss aversion
- Social proof
- Mechanism / proof

### Step 2 — Pick one avatar-situation
Not a demographic — a situation you can put on screen.
- Multi-cat household
- Gone 9–10 hours a day
- Small apartment, indoor-only cat
- Already owns a pet cam
- New kitten, 3am chaos
- Inverted schedule (night shift, sleeps while the cat is awake)
- Destructive cat, real property damage
- Gift buyer (the buyer is not the cat owner — different copy entirely)

### Step 3 — Pick one confirmed feature to carry it
Map from the spec at the top of this document. General pattern:

| Feature type | Best triggers |
|---|---|
| Anything that identifies or personalizes | Identity call-out, curiosity, mechanism |
| Anything technical / sensing | Mechanism, proof, price justification |
| Anything autonomous | Contrast, loss aversion vs. the old way |
| Anything that connects owner to pet remotely | Pain agitation, guilt-repair |

**Never write to a feature that isn't on the confirmed list.** If a headline only works because of a capability the product doesn't have, the headline is wrong, not the product.

### Step 4 — Write the headline
- 4–8 words, uppercase, bold
- Precise over superlative. A specific claim with an implied mechanism beats "the best pet robot ever"
- If it needs a second sentence to land, it's not a headline yet

### Step 5 — Add exactly one emphasis effect
Highlight swipe, underline stroke, strikethrough, or glow. **One per line.** Stacking two on the same words is where renders start smearing.

### Step 6 — Write the primary text with the image, not after
The image opened a question. The copy answers it. Match the copy's job to the ad's stage:
- Hook ads → open with the pain or question, soft CTA
- Explainer ads → explain, then offer
- Closer → lead with the offer

### Guardrails
- **Never two messages in one ad.** If you can't say what job it does in four words, cut something.
- **Price stays off hook and explainer ads.** Closers only.
- **Any discount claim needs a real compare-at live on the PDP** before the ad runs, not after.
- **Never a health or diagnostic claim.** Behavior and routine changes only — "flags when patterns shift," never "detects illness."
- **Generate each prompt twice** and pick the clean render. Or drop the text from the prompt entirely and add it in Canva — more reliable for anything with a price in it.
- **Strikethroughs are the most likely element to render badly.** Add those in Canva.

### Testing rhythm
- 4–6 ads per set. More and each gets too little spend to read.
- Judge on CPA against break-even, not CTR, not which ad got credit.
- Winners fatigue in this account — the pattern has repeated three times. Keep 2–3 challengers in production before the current winner slides.
- Iterate the winner's *structure*; vary the avatar and trigger. Don't rebuild the format that's working.
